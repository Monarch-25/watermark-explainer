In brief — the complete mechanism in three statements.
  • Before generating each token, a hash of the preceding token partitions the vocabulary into a green list (fraction γ) and a red list (fraction 1−γ). Generator and detector derive identical partitions independently — detection requires neither the model weights nor API access.
  • Generation is biased toward green: the hard rule (Algorithm 1) samples exclusively from green; the soft rule (Algorithm 2) adds a δ bias to green logits before sampling.
  • Detection counts green tokens |s|G and applies a one-proportion z-test. Unwatermarked text scores near chance (γT); watermarked text scores far above. The paper rejects at z > 4, a one-sided false-positive rate of ≈ 3×10−5.

01Why watermark model output

As language models produce text at scale — articles, reviews, coursework, social-media content — the ability to distinguish machine-generated from human-written text becomes infrastructure. Watermarking addresses this by embedding a signal at generation time that is imperceptible to readers yet algorithmically recoverable from short spans of text.

Kirchenbauer et al. specify five requirements for a practical scheme: detection without model access; no retraining; detectability from a contiguous fragment; resistance to removal without substantial rewriting; and a rigorous, interpretable confidence measure. The construction below satisfies all five, and its most consequential property is the first — because the detector needs only the hash function and tokenizer, detection can be open-sourced even when the model itself remains proprietary.

This differs fundamentally from post-hoc detection (classifiers trained to spot synthetic text), which degrades as models improve and offers no calibrated confidence. A watermark is a commitment made during sampling, and its statistics are exact under a stated null hypothesis — which is why the resulting p-values can be taken literally.

02Background

2.1  Language models as distributions over tokens

A language model operates over a fixed vocabulary 𝒱 of tokens (words or word fragments; typically |𝒱| ≥ 50,000). Given a prompt and the tokens generated so far, the model's final layer emits a vector of logits ℓ(t) ∈ ℝ|𝒱| — one unnormalized score per vocabulary item. These pass through the softmax operator to form a probability distribution:

pk(t) = exp(ℓk(t)) / ∑i exp(ℓi(t))

The next token s(t) is then drawn from this distribution, most commonly by multinomial sampling (each token with its stated probability), greedy decoding (always the argmax), or beam search (tracking several candidate sequences and keeping the highest-scoring). Watermarking intervenes precisely at this step: it perturbs the logits or restricts the sampling pool before the draw, leaving everything upstream untouched.

A recurring concept is the entropy of the predictive distribution. When probability mass concentrates on a single token (low entropy — memorized facts, boilerplate, formatting), the output is nearly determined and there is no freedom to embed signal without visible distortion. When mass spreads across many plausible tokens (high entropy — open-ended phrasing), the choice among them is essentially free, and a watermark can hide in that freedom. The soft watermark of §3.3 exploits exactly this asymmetry.

2.2  Hypothesis testing in one paragraph

Detection is framed as a null-hypothesis significance test. The null hypothesis H0 states that the examined text was produced with no knowledge of the green lists — i.e., by a human or an unwatermarked model. Under H0, each token lands on the green list with probability γ independently, so the green count follows a binomial distribution, |s|G ∼ Binomial(T, γ), with mean γT and variance Tγ(1−γ). For the sequence lengths of interest, the central limit theorem justifies the normal approximation behind the z-statistic. Observing a z far from zero therefore constitutes evidence against H0, quantified by the p-value: the probability that innocent text would look this incriminating by chance. Two error rates govern the tradeoff: type-I errors (false accusations, controlled by the threshold) and type-II errors (missed watermarks, driven by text entropy and length).

03The watermarking scheme

3.1  Randomized partitioning from hashed context

At step t, both generator and detector compute seed = hash(s(t−1)) and use it to pseudo-randomly split 𝒱 into a green list G of size γ|𝒱| and a red list R of size (1−γ)|𝒱|. Seeding on the preceding token (rather than a global seed) is what makes the partition reproducible without transmitting any side information: the detector replays the same hashes over the text it examines. The paper's reference implementation generalizes this to a window of h preceding tokens; h = 1 here keeps the mechanism legible while preserving all essential statistics.

Hard red list Algorithm 1 · δ → ∞

seed = hash(prev token)
G, R = split(vocab, seed, γ)
sample s(t) from G only

Red-list tokens are assigned zero probability. The signal is maximal — every token is green — and any sequence of 16+ tokens at γ = ½ is conclusive. The cost is quality: on low-entropy passages the rule forbids the only correct continuation (the paper's example: “Obama” following “Barack”), forcing the model into visibly wrong or stilted output.

Soft red list Algorithm 2 · δ ≈ 2

seed = hash(prev token)
G, R = split(vocab, seed, γ)
ℓ̂ = ℓ + δ·𝟙G → softmax → sample

A constant bias δ is added to green logits before the softmax. Dominant logits survive regardless of color; tightly contested races tip green. The watermark thus concentrates itself exactly where it is cheapest — high-entropy positions — and the paper's default δ = 2.0 keeps the perplexity cost near negligible while remaining detectable within roughly 200 tokens.

3.2  Why the soft rule adapts to entropy

Consider a low-entropy step where one token holds nearly all probability mass: its logit exceeds the rest by a large margin, and adding δ to some other token's logit cannot close the gap — the argmax, and usually the sample, is unchanged. At a high-entropy step with many comparable logits, the same δ reshuffles the ranking and the draw lands green with substantially elevated probability. No explicit entropy measurement is required; the arithmetic of the softmax implements the adaptivity. Panel A of the demonstration makes this concrete: switch the entropy regime between “low” and “high” and observe how the post-watermark distribution responds.

04Detection: the z-test

The detector replays the hashes, reconstructs every green list, counts green tokens, and scores:

z = (|s|G − γT) / √(Tγ(1−γ)) · reject H0 if z > z*

Hover each component for its definition. Under H0, z follows approximately a standard normal distribution; the denominator is exactly the binomial standard deviation.

Worked example (paper, Figure 1; γ = 0.25, δ = 2). A watermarked passage contains T = 36 scored tokens. Chance alone predicts γT = 9 greens, yet 28 are observed: z = (28−9)/√(36·0.25·0.75) ≈ 7.3, corresponding to p ≈ 6×10−14 — a one-in-ten-trillion coincidence under the null. This is the sense in which detection carries “interpretable p-values”: the number means what it says.

Threshold selection. The paper standard z* = 4 admits a one-sided false-positive rate of ≈ 3×10−5 (roughly one false accusation per 30,000 innocent passages). Raising z* buys near-immunity to false accusations at the price of missed short or low-entropy watermarked passages; lowering it does the reverse. Panel C lets you move z* and watch both sides of this tradeoff shift in real time.

06Analysis: sensitivity, quality, and parameters

6.1  Sensitivity and spike entropy

Detection power is not a property of the watermark alone — it depends on how much freedom the text afforded. The paper formalizes “freedom” as spike entropy, S(p, z) = ∑k pk/(1+zpk): a soft count of the tokens holding non-trivial mass. Theorem 4.2 converts average spike entropy S* over a passage into a lower bound on the expected green count:

Theorem 4.2 (informal) — Kirchenbauer et al. With α = eδ, the expected number of green tokens satisfies 𝔼|s|G ≥ [γαT / (1+(α−1)γ)]·S*. High-entropy passages (S* near 1) approach the fully-shifted rate; low-entropy passages guarantee little — correctly, since there was nothing to randomize. Panel A reports per-step spike entropy so this bound can be read off the distributions directly.

Empirically (§4.1, OPT-1.3B, T = 200, γ = 0.5, δ = 2): mean spike entropy 0.807, mean green count 159.5 against a theoretical floor of 142.2, and 98.4% sensitivity at z* = 4 under multinomial sampling (99.6% with 4-way beam search, which “irons in” green-dense candidates). The residual misses concentrate in memorized or templated passages — the failure mode reproduced here by the “low” entropy regime.

6.2  Effect on text quality

Quality is measured by perplexity under a larger reference model. Theorem 4.3 bounds the expected perplexity increase uniformly: at the extremes it vanishes (uniform distributions sample uniformly regardless of coloring; degenerate distributions are unaffected by δ), with the residual cost concentrated at moderate entropy. The demonstration's mean total-variation distance plays the analogous role for the abstract distributions: it quantifies, per step, how far the sampler moved. A robust finding, visible in the paper's Figure 2 and reproducible above: raising δ past ≈ 2 increases distortion steadily while detection saturates — the signature of entropy-limited headroom.

6.3  Parameter guidance

γ (green fraction). Smaller γ lowers the chance baseline, making a given elevation more surprising — the paper finds γ = 0.1–0.25 slightly Pareto-optimal for the strength–quality frontier, though anything in 0.25–0.5 behaves reasonably. Verify this: set γ = 0.1 with δ = 2 and compare detection against γ = 0.5 at matched distortion.

δ (bias strength). The operating band is roughly [0.5, 2.0] for capable base models; overconfident (e.g., instruction-tuned) distributions may warrant larger values. δ → ∞ recovers the hard rule and its quality penalties — instructive to inspect, inadvisable to deploy.

T and z*. Longer passages and lower thresholds both raise power at the cost of latency and false accusations respectively. The paper's z* = 4 with T ≈ 200 is a calibrated default, not a law; short-form settings (a synthetic reply of 30 tokens) demand either stronger δ or acceptance of weaker guarantees.

07Robustness, attacks, and private watermarking

The z-test formulation already buys substantial tamper-resistance: because greens are counted over the whole passage, an adversary must rewrite roughly a quarter of the tokens to drag z below threshold — and each edited token disturbs the hash chain of its successor, compounding the attacker's burden (§2, “How hard is it to remove the watermark?”).

The paper catalogs three attack families. Insertion and deletion add or remove tokens (wasting generation budget and narrowing effective context). Substitution — manual paraphrasing or automated rewriting with a weaker public model — trades watermark strength against fluency, and is bounded by a simple economic argument: an attacker possessing an equally strong paraphraser has little need for the watermarked API. Generative attacks (emoji interleaving, homoglyph substitution, instructed character swaps) abuse in-context learning to perturb tokenization predictably; these are the strongest known tools, and the recommended mitigations are canonicalization before hashing and moderate hash windows (h = 2–4).

Finally, the scheme supports a private mode: the partition seed derives from a keyed pseudorandom function whose key is held server-side, with detection served behind a rate-limited API. Brute-force recovery of the lists then requires |𝒱|1+h queries — infeasible for h ≫ 1 — at the cost of trusting the API operator. Algorithm 3 of the paper further removes “attack amplification” (where one edit randomizes many downstream lists) via a self-hash construction.

08Limitations and scope of this demonstration

Three deliberate simplifications bound what the demonstration shows. First, the generative distributions are synthetic Gaussians, not a trained model: entropy regimes are stipulated rather than emergent, so absolute quality claims (perplexity, fluency) cannot transfer — only the mechanism (partitioning, shifting, scoring) is exact. Second, the hash window is h = 1 with a non-cryptographic mixer, whereas deployments use keyed PRFs over wider windows with repeated-n-gram handling. Third, adversarial dynamics (paraphrasing, tokenization attacks, multiple-key privacy) are analyzed in prose, not simulated. None of these affect the statistical core: the z-test evaluated here is term-for-term the paper's Equation 3, and its calibration can be checked independently via the companion script (conda activate torch, then python watermark_torch.py).

On the fundamental limit (§1.2). Low-entropy text — memorized facts, canonical code, rigid templates — is barely watermarkable in principle: when model and human must emit identical tokens, no statistical test can separate them. The soft rule's virtue is graceful behavior in exactly this regime: it declines to distort what it cannot mark, provided such passages are embedded in enough total entropy elsewhere.
